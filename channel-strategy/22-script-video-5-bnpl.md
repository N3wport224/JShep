## COLD OPEN — HOOK (0:00–0:35) — "The Painless Button" style

**VO:** There's a button that's been quietly added to almost every checkout page on the internet.
It takes any price — shoes, groceries, concert tickets, a couch — and splits it into four painless little pieces. No interest, it says. No catch, it implies.
That button is now moving hundreds of billions of dollars a year. It's the fastest-growing form of borrowing on Earth.
And here's the strange part: most of it is invisible. Not to you — to the *machines.* The scoring machine we opened two videos ago? For years, it mostly couldn't see this debt at all.

## ACT 1 — HOW THE TRICK WORKS (0:35–3:05)

**VO:** First, the concession — because the pitch is genuinely good. Pay-in-four, no interest, no fees if you're on time. Compared to a credit card charging twenty-four percent? For a disciplined user, it really can be the cheaper tool. This machine didn't grow by lying.
It grew by moving the cost somewhere you can't see it.

[TEXT: "The pitch is true. The price is hidden."]

**VO:** Here's the gear that makes "free" possible: you're not the one paying for it. The *store* is. Every time you split a purchase, the BNPL company charges the merchant a fee — several times what credit cards charge them. Merchants pay it happily. Why? Because the button works: people buy more, buy bigger, and abandon fewer carts when the price is chopped into quarters.

[GFX: three-way flow — YOU (pays $0 extra) · STORE (pays fee slice to) · BNPL MACHINE; store's slice visibly larger than a card-swipe fee]

**VO:** Read that again from the store's side. They're paying a premium fee for one service: making prices feel smaller than they are. The machine's actual product isn't credit. It's *anesthesia.* The ratchet from last video raises the price — this button numbs it.

⏱ [EDIT: thesis beat, music thins:]
[TEXT: "The product is painlessness."]

**VO:** And it works on the mind exactly as designed. A hundred-twenty-dollar price tag triggers a spending decision. Four thirties barely registers as one. Stack a few of those a week — each one reasonable, none of them *felt* — and the total is something you never actually decided.

[B-ROLL: rapid-fire checkout pops filling a screen like notifications; a monthly ledger quietly assembling in the corner]

## ACT 2 — THE BLIND SPOT (3:05–5:30)

**VO:** Now connect it to the machines you already know. When you take a credit card, the lender pulls your file, the loan appears in your report, the scoring machine watches your balance. The system sees you.
Pay-in-four grew up outside all of that. For most of its history: no hard credit check to sign up, and — here's the stunner — most of these loans were never reported to the bureaus at all.

[GFX: the credit-score conveyor from Video 3; BNPL purchases flying *over* the bureau vaults, never landing]

⏱ **VO:** Which means a person could hold five, ten, fifteen of these loans at once — across four different apps — and every one of those companies, plus every *other* lender, sees none of it. There's a name for taking new loans to stay afloat on old ones: loan stacking. The oldest failure mode in lending. The exact thing credit files were invented to catch.

[EDIT: full stop. Silence beat:]
[TEXT: "Everyone's lending. No one's counting."]

**VO:** That's why regulators and economists started using the phrase *phantom debt* — billions in consumer borrowing that official statistics couldn't fully see. It's starting to change: the bureaus and FICO have begun building BNPL into files and scores, and rules treating pay-in-four like real credit have been fought over for years. But "starting to" is doing a lot of work in that sentence. Today, the fog is still the defining feature.

## ACT 3 — THE STORY: WHERE WE'VE SEEN THIS MOVIE (5:30–7:55) ⏱

**VO:** Small loans. Eager lenders. No shared visibility. Borrowers stacking obligations nobody totals up. If that pattern sounds familiar, it's because it has a track record.
Two thousand six. Mortgage lenders competing to lend to anyone, checks getting lighter every quarter, each lender seeing only its own slice — and the total, the *system-wide* total, effectively invisible until it wasn't.

[B-ROLL: 2006-era open-house signs, stacks of loan files → archival 2008 footage]
[EDIT: *Pulse* cue]

**VO:** Now — deep breath — the concession, because it matters: this is *not* 2008. The loans are tiny. They're weeks long, not thirty years. There's no trillion-dollar bond machine repackaging sneaker installments. If BNPL blows up, it doesn't take the banking system with it.
Here's what *should* worry you instead. Surveys keep finding the heaviest users are the people with the least slack — younger, lower-income, already revolving credit-card debt — using the button for groceries and essentials. Late payments among users have been climbing. And when a paycheck breaks, BNPL is the debt people skip *first* — it has the least memory. The machine doesn't threaten the economy. It threatens *its own users*, quietly, one reasonable-looking thirty-dollar slice at a time.

[GFX: a single user's month assembling: 11 active plans, staggered due dates forming a near-daily drumbeat calendar]
[TEXT: "Not a system risk. A *you* risk."]

**VO:** And the tell is in the product design itself: autopay by default, due dates scattered across the month, four apps with four totals and no statement that ever shows you the sum. The credit card at least sends you one terrifying number every month. This machine's genius is that *no such number exists.*

⏱ [EDIT: music out. One line on black:]
[TEXT: "No bill. No total. No moment of truth."]

## ACT 4 — USING THE BUTTON WITHOUT BEING USED (7:55–9:35)

**VO:** So: better mousetrap or baby subprime? Honest answer — it's a genuinely useful tool bolted onto a genuinely dangerous dashboard. Four moves. ⏱

[EDIT: *Warm* cue]

**VO:** One: recreate the moment of truth the machine deleted. One note on your phone — every active plan, every remaining payment, one total. If writing the total down changes how you feel, the anesthesia was working.
Two: one app, never several. Stacking across providers is the exact blind spot; don't personally rebuild 2006 in your pocket.
Three: never split essentials. Groceries on installments isn't a hack, it's a smoke alarm — the signal to cut, earn, or restructure, not to numb.
Four: assume it all counts, because soon it will. The bureaus are wiring this in; the era of consequence-free missed slices is closing. Treat every pay-in-four like it reports to the scoring machine — the one that prices you for a decade.

[GFX: checklist ×4 — the "one total" note · single-app icon · red X over grocery split · conveyor finally connecting BNPL into the bureau vaults]

## CLOSE + SEQUEL LOOP (9:35–10:10)

**VO:** Five machines in, a pattern: every machine we've opened works by making sure you never see one honest total. The bank shows a balance that isn't money. The ratchet hides the climb. The button deletes the bill.
But what happens when the totals win anyway? When someone — maybe through no fault of their own — just… stops paying? Everyone assumes they know: disaster, ruin, end of story. The real machinery of default is stranger, slower, and more negotiable than almost anyone realizes — and knowing how it actually works is the difference between a bad year and a lost decade. That's the next video.
No hype. Just the gears.
