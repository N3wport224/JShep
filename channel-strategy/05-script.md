**Target runtime:** ~10:30 of voiceover (~1,550 words @ ~150 wpm) · **Format:** VO + stock footage + motion graphics · **Tone:** calm, forensic, dry wit — never outraged, never hype.

## COLD OPEN — THE HOOK (0:00–0:35)

**VO:** Tomorrow morning, walk into your bank and ask for everything in your account. In cash. They'll probably give it to you.
Now imagine everyone at your branch does the same thing, on the same day.
The doors close by noon. Not because the bank is broken — because it was never holding your money in the first place.

[B-ROLL: bank doors being unlocked in morning light → hands counting bills → crowd time-lapse at ATMs]
[TEXT: "by noon." — hard cut to black on the word]
[SFX: ambient bank noise cuts to silence on "noon"]

**VO:** Your account balance isn't money sitting in a vault. It's a number the bank *owes* you. And what they actually do with your paycheck the moment it lands is one of the strangest, oldest, and most profitable machines ever built.
Today we're opening it up.

[GFX: account balance "$4,217.66" dissolves into the words "IOU — Your Bank"]
[EDIT: first music cue starts here — low, ticking, mechanical]

## ACT 1 — WHERE YOUR MONEY ACTUALLY GOES (0:35–3:00)

**VO:** First, a concession — because I know what some of you are thinking. This isn't a scam, and your bank isn't stealing. Everything you're about to see is legal, public, and roughly three hundred years old. That's what makes it interesting.

[TEXT: "legal · public · ~300 years old"]

**VO:** Here's the machine. You deposit a thousand dollars. The bank is required to keep only a slice of it ready for withdrawals. The rest? It's lent out — to someone's mortgage, someone's car loan, someone's small business — usually within days.

[GFX: $1,000 splits — thin slice labeled "reserves" stays in a vault icon; the rest flows through pipes into "MORTGAGE," "AUTO LOAN," "BUSINESS"]

**VO:** And here's the part almost nobody knows. In the United States, since March 2020, the required slice is officially… zero percent.

⏱ [EDIT: full stop. One second of silence. Zoom-punch on the graphic.]
[TEXT: "Required reserves: 0%" — stark, centered]

**VO:** Zero. The rule most of us vaguely remember from school — "banks keep ten percent" — was quietly retired. Banks hold reserves because it's prudent, and because regulators watch other numbers instead. But the law forcing them to hold a fixed slice of *your* deposit? Gone.

[B-ROLL: archive footage of empty vault; Federal Reserve building]

**VO:** So if your money's been lent to a stranger's mortgage, what's in your account? A promise. A very reliable promise, most of the time. Your balance is an entry in the bank's ledger that says: *we owe you this much, on demand.*
You're not a customer with a storage locker. You're a lender. Congratulations — you've been in the banking business this whole time. Your interest rate? You might want to sit down.

[GFX: ledger animation — "Bank owes: YOU… $4,217.66"]
[TEXT: "You are the lender." ]
[B-ROLL: person checking a 0.01% APY statement, slow zoom]

## ACT 2 — THE MACHINE MAKES MONEY (3:00–5:30)

**VO:** Now it gets stranger. Because when a bank lends your deposit out… your account doesn't go down. You still see your full balance. And the borrower sees their new loan. Which means — count it — there is now *more money* in the system than there was a minute ago.

[GFX: $1,000 becomes $1,000 (your account) + $900 (borrower's account) — total counter ticks up. Label the held-back $100 "prudence, not law" — this keeps the example consistent with the 0% requirement established at 1:45]

**VO:** This is the machine's real secret: most money isn't printed. It's *loaned into existence.* Somewhere over ninety percent of the money you'll ever touch was created not by a government press, but by a commercial bank making a loan.

⏱ [EDIT: register change — music drops out, single piano note. This is the video's thesis.]
[TEXT: "Money is loaned into existence."]

**VO:** When you repay the loan, that money is un-created. Destroyed. The money supply literally breathes — expanding when banks are confident, contracting when they're scared. Which means the amount of money that *exists* depends, in part, on the mood of loan officers.

[GFX: money-supply "lungs" inhaling/exhaling with a confidence meter]
[B-ROLL: office workers, loan documents being signed, then shredded]

**VO:** And the bank's profit is the gap. They pay you almost nothing to hold your promise, charge the borrower seven, eight, twenty-four percent, and keep the difference. It's called the spread, and it is one of the most reliable business models ever invented. Not a heist. A toll booth.

[GFX: toll booth on a bridge of flowing cash — "0.01% in → 8% out"]

## ACT 3 — WHEN THE MACHINE JAMS (5:30–8:00) ⏱ *(narrative centerpiece at the retention trough)*

**VO:** So what happens when everyone asks for their promise back at once?
March 2023. Silicon Valley Bank. Sixteenth largest bank in America. On Wednesday, it's fine. By Friday, it doesn't exist.

[B-ROLL: news footage montage, SVB signage, phones with banking apps]
[EDIT: cut pace doubles; music switches to pulse]

**VO:** Here's the 48-hour anatomy. SVB had done the normal thing — lent depositors' money out, much of it locked in long-term bonds. Then a few large depositors got nervous and hit withdraw. Word spread — not through bank lobbies, but through group chats. Forty-two billion dollars asked to leave in a single day. No bank on Earth holds that slice.

[GFX: timeline bar Wed→Fri filling red; counter to "$42,000,000,000 / day"]
[TEXT: "A bank run is a rumor with an app."]

**VO:** The machine's one fatal flaw is that it runs on belief. Every bank, even a healthy one, dies if enough people stop believing on the same afternoon. That's not a bug from 2023 — it's the same failure as 1929. The app just made the line move faster.

[B-ROLL: 1930s bank-run archival footage cut against modern phone taps — match-cut the queues]

**VO:** Which is why the machine has a safety system bolted on: deposit insurance. In the US, the FDIC guarantees your deposits up to two hundred fifty thousand dollars per bank, per depositor — a promise backed by the one entity that can't run out of dollars. It exists for exactly one reason: so you never feel the need to join the line. The insurance works best when it's never used.

⏱ [GFX: FDIC shield stamping onto the account ledger — "$250,000" ]
[TEXT: "The line only forms if you believe it will."]

## ACT 4 — WHAT THIS MEANS FOR YOU (8:00–9:45)

**VO:** So — should you be worried? Mostly, no. And now you know *why* "mostly."

[EDIT: tone lightens; music warms]

**VO:** Three things you now see that most people don't.
One: your balance is a loan you're making, so the real question about any bank isn't "is my money there?" — it never is — it's "is this bank insured, and am I under the limit?" If you're over two-fifty at one bank, that's not extra safety. That's an uninsured loan to a company you've never audited.

[GFX: checklist item 1 — account splitting across two bank icons]

**VO:** Two: banks pay you almost nothing because most people never ask for more. The spread is their business — but *which* bank earns your deposit is the one lever you actually hold.

[GFX: checklist item 2 — 0.01% vs 4%+ side-by-side]

**VO:** Three: when you hear "the Fed raised rates," you now know what's really being adjusted — the price of the machine's fuel. Rates up: loans slow, money-creation slows, everything cools. Rates down: the lungs inhale. Every headline about the Fed is a headline about *this* machine.

[GFX: master schematic returns — Fed dial connected to the gears]
