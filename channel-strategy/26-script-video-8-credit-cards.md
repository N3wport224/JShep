## ACT 1 — THE SWIPE (0:35–3:05)

**VO:** Start with the part everyone gets wrong: the network on the front of your card — the famous logos — mostly isn't lending you anything. They run the *rails.* The money comes from the issuing bank behind the card. So when you swipe, here's the two seconds you never see:

[GFX: swipe → route lights up: STORE → store's bank → NETWORK RAILS → your bank → approved. Round trip stopwatch: ~2s]

**VO:** The store doesn't receive your full payment. A slice — generally somewhere around two to three percent, more for premium cards — is skimmed off before the money lands: the interchange fee, most of it flowing to the bank that issued your card. Every swipe, every store, every day. In the US, that skim adds up to *over a hundred billion dollars a year.*
The concession, because it's real: those rails are a genuine marvel — fraud screening, instant settlement, worldwide acceptance, a dispute system where the bank fights the store on your behalf. The machine earns *something.* The question is who's paying it — and the answer is where this gets uncomfortable.

[GFX: a $100 purchase splitting: store keeps ~$97.50, the skim fans out — issuing bank (largest), network, processor]
[TEXT: "The rails are real. So is the skim."]

⏱ **VO:** Because stores don't eat that fee. They price it in — into the sticker price *everyone* pays, including people paying cash. Now follow the loop: card users get rewards funded by interchange… which is baked into prices… which cash payers also pay… and cash payers skew lower-income. Researchers at the Federal Reserve Bank of Boston traced the whole circle and put it plainly: on net, cash users subsidize card users' rewards — money flowing, quietly, from poorer households to richer ones, a few cents per transaction, billions of times a day.

[EDIT: full stop. Silence beat:]
[TEXT: "Your points are funded by people who don't have points."]

## ACT 2 — THE TWO CUSTOMERS (3:05–5:35)

**VO:** Now the machine's core secret: it runs two businesses on the same card, and which business *you're* in depends on one behavior.
Customer type one: the transactor. Pays the statement in full, every month. Collects rewards, pays no interest, rides the grace period — that quiet miracle where the bank lends you money for up to seven weeks at zero percent. Transactors are barely profitable; some are net losses. The industry has a name for them — and it's not a compliment: *deadbeats.*

⏱ [TEXT: "In this industry, the customer who always pays… is the 'deadbeat.'"]
[GFX: two doors on the machine: DOOR 1 — rewards fountain, "profit: thin"]

**VO:** Customer type two: the revolver. Carries a balance — any balance. And here's the gear almost nobody knows: the moment you revolve, the grace period *dies.* Not just on the old balance — on *new* purchases too, which start accruing interest the day you make them, until you've fully paid off and often waited a full cycle. Revolve once, and the zero-percent miracle quietly switches off while the app looks exactly the same.

[GFX: DOOR 2 — the grace-period light snapping off; new purchases sprouting interest meters instantly]

**VO:** And what rate greets you behind door two? The average card now sits above twenty percent — among the most expensive mainstream borrowing that exists, on the same card that pays door-one customers to use it. Then comes the minimum payment: engineered to feel responsible while barely denting the balance — pay minimums on a typical balance at these rates and you can be paying for *decades*, handing over more in interest than you borrowed. Your statement is legally required to show you that math in a little box. The box exists because Congress had to force it.

[GFX: minimum-payment box from a real statement blown up: "If you make only the minimum… ~20+ years"; a coin conveyor: rewards chute (small) vs interest pipe (massive — the machine's real revenue)]
[TEXT: "Interest, not swipes, is the engine."]

## ACT 3 — THE STORY: WHY YOUR CARD LIVES IN SOUTH DAKOTA (5:35–8:00) ⏱

**VO:** Which raises the historical question: how is twenty-plus percent even legal? For most of American history, it wasn't. States had usury laws — hard caps on interest rates, some of the oldest consumer protections on the books. So what happened to them?
A court case almost nobody's heard of, and a deal cut in a state most people couldn't place on a map.

[B-ROLL: dusty statute books; 1970s bank branches; archival Minneapolis/Omaha footage]
[EDIT: *Pulse* cue]

**VO:** Nineteen seventy-eight. A tiny lawsuit — Marquette National Bank versus First of Omaha — reaches the Supreme Court with a technical question: when a bank in one state lends to a customer in another, whose interest-rate cap applies? The Court's answer, unanimous: the *bank's* home state. Not yours.
Read that again, because the entire modern card industry lives inside it: a bank chartered in a state with *no* rate cap can lend at any rate, to anyone, in all fifty states — and the borrower's local usury law simply doesn't apply.

[GFX: a cap labeled "YOUR STATE: 12%" dissolving as the loan's tag switches to "BANK'S STATE: ∞"]
[TEXT: "1978: the caps stopped following the borrower."]

**VO:** The race started immediately. Nineteen-eighty: Citibank's card business is drowning — double-digit inflation, remember the ratchet? — while New York's cap strangles what it can charge. South Dakota, farm economy struggling, makes an offer: move here, we'll erase our cap entirely. Citibank moves four hundred jobs to Sioux Falls. Delaware runs the same play a year later. Within a few years, the states with no caps *are* the credit card industry — which is why, to this day, the return address on your statement is so often Sioux Falls or Wilmington.

⏱ [EDIT: music out. One line on black:]
[TEXT: "The rate on your card was set by a race between two small states."]

**VO:** No villain twirled a mustache here — that's what makes it the perfect machine story. A court answered a technical question. Two struggling states competed for jobs. Banks did arithmetic. And a protection that had existed since before the founding evaporated for an entire country, without a single vote on the question itself.

## ACT 4 — PICKING YOUR DOOR (8:00–9:55)

**VO:** So the machine has two doors, and it profits most when you drift between them without noticing. Four moves. ⏱

[EDIT: *Warm* cue]

**VO:** One: know your door — out loud. If you carried *any* balance last month, you are a revolver, the grace period is off, and rewards are a rounding error against twenty-plus percent. Until the balance is zero, the only reward worth chasing is the death of that balance.
Two: if you revolve, stop swiping that card. Because grace is dead, every new purchase bleeds from day one. Move daily spending to debit or a second card paid in full, and let the wounded card heal without new cuts.
Three: use the machine's own weapons on it. Zero-percent balance-transfer offers exist because issuers poach each other's revolvers — a transfer fee of a few percent can replace twenty-plus percent for over a year of runway. It only works with a payoff plan and no new spending; otherwise you've just added a fee to the fire.
Four: transactors — enjoy door one with clear eyes. Autopay the *full statement*, never the minimum; take the rewards; and remember what you learned today about who ultimately funds them. The machine calls you a deadbeat. Wear it proudly.

## CLOSE — SEASON CAP + SEQUEL LOOP (9:55–10:40)

**VO:** Eight machines. Your bank lends your deposits into existence. The Fed prices every dollar. A bureau scores you and sells the score. A ratchet keeps prices climbing. A button hides the total. A timeline governs what happens when you can't pay. A pool prices your disasters. And a card runs two businesses on your behavior.
See it now? It was never separate machines. It's one machine — and every gear meshes with the next.
Which leaves the biggest gear of all, the one this whole series has been circling: the largest purchase of your life, the debt that takes thirty years, and the strange truth that you don't really buy a house at all — you rent money, at a scale that reshapes everything else you'll ever do. That's where the machine goes next.
No hype. Just the gears.
