## ACT 1 — WHAT THE MACHINE ACTUALLY IS (0:35–3:05)

**VO:** First, the concession — because this machine exists for a reason. Before credit scores, a loan officer looked you up and down and decided if you *seemed* trustworthy. Which meant the wrong name, the wrong neighborhood, the wrong face — no loan. The score, born in 1989, replaced a biased gut with a blind formula. That part was progress. The formula doesn't know your race, your gender, or your salary.
What it became is something else: a three-company industry that files your entire financial life without asking.

[B-ROLL: 1960s bank office, loan officer archival → cut to server farm]
[TEXT: "1989: the gut gets replaced"]

**VO:** Here's the assembly line. Every lender you touch — card, car, student loan — reports your behavior monthly to three private bureaus: Equifax, Experian, TransUnion. The bureaus compile the file. Then a fourth company — FICO — runs its formula over that file and stamps out the number. Bureaus sell the file. FICO licenses the formula. Lenders buy the output. Billions of scores are sold every year.
Notice who's missing from every transaction in that chain. You. You're in *all* of the products and *none* of the deals.

[GFX: conveyor: LENDERS → report → 3 BUREAU vaults → file → FICO stamping press → "715" → sold to buyer icons (bank, landlord, insurer)]
[SFX: ratchet-click per handoff]

**VO:** And the number itself? Somewhere between 300 and 850. The national average right now: about 715 — and for the first time in a decade, it's falling.

⏱ **VO:** But here's the stat that breaks people's brains: you don't have *a* credit score. There are dozens of FICO versions, plus a competing formula the bureaus built themselves, plus special editions just for car lenders and mortgage lenders. The number you check in your banking app? Probably not the one your mortgage lender sees.

[EDIT: full stop, silence beat]
[TEXT: "There is no 'your credit score.' There are dozens."]

## ACT 2 — THE RECIPE, AND WHAT IT CAN'T SEE (3:05–5:35)

**VO:** So what's in the formula? FICO publishes the recipe in broad strokes. Thirty-five percent: do you pay on time. Thirty percent: how much of your available credit you're using. Fifteen: how long you've had credit. Ten: how recently you've asked for more. Ten: the mix of types.

[GFX: recipe card / gauge cluster — five dials filling to 35/30/15/10/10]

**VO:** Now flip it over and look at what's *not* in there. Your income. Your savings. Your rent, most of the time. Your net worth. A millionaire who's never borrowed can score worse than someone juggling five cards — because this machine doesn't measure wealth, or even responsibility. It measures one thing: **how you handle owing people money.**

⏱ [EDIT: thesis on black, slowest read:]
[TEXT: "It's not a trust score. It's a debt-handling score."]

**VO:** Which produces the machine's strangest incentive: the formula quietly rewards people who stay mildly in debt forever, and it simply cannot see the fifty million Americans with files too thin to score at all — "credit invisibles," locked out not for failing the test, but for never having been allowed to take it.
And one more gear most people never notice: utilization — that thirty percent slice — is a *snapshot*, not a habit. The machine photographs your balance whenever the lender happens to report. Max the card and pay it in full a week later, and the photo may still show you at ninety percent. You did nothing wrong. The camera just fired at the wrong moment.

[GFX: camera-shutter freeze-frame on a balance bar at 90%; calendar showing report date ≠ payment date]

## ACT 3 — THE STORY: WHEN THE FILING CABINET LEAKED (5:35–7:55) ⏱

**VO:** September 2017. Equifax — one of the three companies holding your file — announces a breach. Not passwords. Not one bank's customers. The *files themselves*: names, birth dates, Social Security numbers, addresses. A hundred and forty-seven million people. Roughly half of American adults.

[B-ROLL: news montage, congressional hearing footage]
[EDIT: *Pulse* cue; pace up]
[GFX: counter to 147,000,000; US map filling]

**VO:** Here's what made it different from every other hack: not one of those 147 million people was an Equifax customer. They never signed up. There was no account to close, no service to quit. The raw material can't fire the factory.
The company took forty days to tell the public. Executives sold stock in between — investigators later cleared most of it, but one manager went to prison for insider trading. The eventual settlement offered victims free credit monitoring… much of it run by the same industry that leaked the data.

[TEXT: "You can't quit a system you never joined."]
[EDIT: music thins to heartbeat on that line]

**VO:** Congress did do one real thing: since 2018, freezing your file — locking the factory door so no new lender can pull it — is free, at all three bureaus, by law. Remember that. It's the single most useful lever in this entire video, and it exists because the machine jammed publicly.

[GFX: three vault doors, each getting a padlock — "FREE · by law · since 2018"]

## ACT 4 — WORKING THE MACHINE (7:55–9:40) ⏱

**VO:** So — can you win a game you never signed up for? Mostly, yes. Four moves.

[EDIT: *Warm* cue]

**VO:** One: the two big dials are the only dials. Pay on time, every time — autopay the minimum as a floor — and keep reported utilization low. Those two levers are sixty-five percent of the formula. Everything else is rounding.
Two: beat the camera. If a big balance is about to be photographed, pay it down *before* the statement closes, not just by the due date. Same spending, different photo.
Three: never close your oldest card without a reason. You'd be shredding your own longest evidence.
Four: your file is free to read — every week, from all three bureaus, at the one government-mandated site — and free to freeze. Anyone charging you monthly to "protect" or "boost" your score is selling you back your own data with a bow on it.

[GFX: checklist ×4; dial cluster re-highlighting 35+30; scissors hovering over old card → red X]

## CLOSE + SEQUEL LOOP (9:40–10:20)

**VO:** Step back and look at the machine we just opened. It watches what you owe, photographs you at random, stamps you into a number, and sells you — billions of times a year — to decide what you pay for money.
But what you pay for money is only half your bill. The other half is what money *buys.* And that number has been doing something strange: it went up, everyone got angry, the anger faded — and the prices stayed. That's not a malfunction. Next time: the machine that makes sure prices never come back down.
No hype. Just the gears.
