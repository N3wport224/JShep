**VO:** Eight times a year, about a dozen people walk into a marble building in Washington, close the door, and talk for two days.
When they come out, they announce one number.
That number decides what your credit card charges you, what your savings account pays you, whether your boss expands or does layoffs — and whether you can afford a house this decade.
You didn't vote for them. Most people can't name one of them. And the strangest part? The machine they operate mostly works by *announcing things.*

## ACT 1 — WHAT THIS THING EVEN IS (0:35–3:10)

**VO:** First, the concession: the Fed is not a secret cabal, and it's not "the government printing money." The truth is weirder — it's both public and private, and technically neither.

[TEXT: "not a cabal · not exactly government · weirder"]

**VO:** Congress built it in 1913, after a financial panic so bad it was stopped by *one rich guy.* In 1907, banks were collapsing in a chain reaction, there was no Fed — so J.P. Morgan, a private citizen, locked the heads of the biggest banks in his personal library overnight and refused to let them out until they pledged money to stop the panic. It worked. And it terrified everyone. Because the entire American economy had just been saved by the mood of one man.

⏱ [B-ROLL: 1907 archival photos, Morgan portrait, the actual Morgan Library]
[TEXT: "1907: bailed out by one man's library"]
[EDIT: story register — this is the act-1 mini-narrative; music family *Pulse*, low]

**VO:** So Congress built a permanent version of Morgan's library: a lender that could never run out, for the system we saw in the last video — where every bank is one bad afternoon away from a line out the door. Twelve regional Reserve banks, a board in Washington, and a committee — the FOMC — where a dozen participants meet eight times a year, and the voting seats decide that one number.

[GFX: US map — 12 regional bank nodes wiring into DC; committee table forms; "8 meetings/year"]

**VO:** The number is the federal funds rate. And here's the first surprise: it's not a rate the Fed charges *you.* It's the rate banks charge *each other* to borrow overnight — remember, they're all lending your deposits out, and some of them end the day short. The Fed sets the price of that overnight scramble. That's it. That's the dial.

[GFX: two bank icons passing money at 3 AM; dial labeled "FED FUNDS RATE" — callback link to Video 1's machine]

## ACT 2 — HOW ONE DIAL REACHES YOUR WALLET (3:10–5:40)

**VO:** So how does the price of a loan between two banks at 3 AM become the price of *your* life? It flows downhill, in order.

[GFX: waterfall — dial at top, cascading levels below]

**VO:** Banks that pay more to borrow overnight charge more for everything downstream. Credit cards float a few points above it. Car loans price off it. Mortgages track the bond market's *bet* on where the dial goes next — which is why mortgage rates move on the Fed's *words* before it touches anything.

⏱ [GFX: waterfall levels fill: "CARDS +X%," "AUTO," "MORTGAGE," each with ratchet-click SFX]

**VO:** And that's the part nobody tells you: the machine's main tool is *talking.* Markets obsess over every syllable the Fed Chair says, because trillions of dollars reprice on the hint. A machine that works because everyone believes it will — where have we heard that before?

[B-ROLL: press conference, traders watching screens; freeze frame on a single word of a statement]
[TEXT: "The dial is a sentence."]

**VO:** When the dial goes up, borrowing slows, businesses postpone, hiring cools — and from the last video, you know the deeper gear: fewer loans means less new money *being created at all.* The lungs exhale. When the dial goes down, the lungs inhale. One committee, adjusting the breathing rate of the entire money supply.

[GFX: Video 1's money-lungs graphic returns, now connected to the dial — breathing speeds/slows as dial turns]

## ACT 3 — THE WEEKEND THE DIAL WASN'T ENOUGH (5:40–8:10) ⏱ *(story centerpiece at the trough)*

**VO:** March 2020. The world is locking down. Markets aren't falling — they're *seizing.* Even US government bonds — the thing everything else is priced on — can't find buyers. The plumbing is jammed.

[B-ROLL: empty streets, trading floor chaos, red screens]
[EDIT: music *Pulse* full; cut pace doubles]

**VO:** On a Sunday night — before markets even open — the Fed slams the dial to zero. Emergency announcement. And it isn't enough. So the machine reveals its second mode: it starts *buying.* Bonds, by the hundreds of billions, paid for with dollars that did not exist until the moment of purchase. Typed into being. In the following months, the Fed's balance sheet grows by roughly three trillion dollars.

[GFX: dial spins to 0% with vault-thunk; then a counter: balance sheet ~$4T → $7T+, timeline compressing months]
[TEXT: "dollars, typed into being"]

**VO:** If that sounds like the "money printer" meme — this is where the meme comes from, and also where it's wrong. Those new dollars mostly became bank reserves — fuel in the tanks of the machine from Video 1 — not cash mailed to your door. But fuel matters. Two years later, inflation hit a forty-year high, and economists are still fighting about how much of it this machine caused.

⏱ [EDIT: music out. One line on black:]
[TEXT: "The machine can type money. It cannot type stuff."]

**VO:** And when the Fed reversed it — dial up faster than any time since the 1980s — you felt *that* too: mortgages tripled, tech layoffs, your savings account suddenly paying interest for the first time in fifteen years. One dial. Both directions. Nobody's exempt.

[GFX: dial cranks up 2022–2023; waterfall levels refill with higher numbers]

## ACT 4 — WHAT YOU DO WITH THIS (8:10–9:55)

**VO:** So what do you do with a machine you can't vote on and can't opt out of? Three things. ⏱

[EDIT: *Warm* cue; bright schematic]

**VO:** One: stop reading Fed headlines as noise. "Rates held" or "cut expected" is a forecast for *your* life — what you'll earn on savings, what refinancing will cost, how safe your job is next year. You now know which gear moves first.
Two: the dial cuts both ways, so position both ways. When the dial is high, cash finally pays — that's when a high-yield account beats the 0.01% from Video 1. When it's falling, locking borrowing costs early is the whole game.
Three: never bet against the dial. Whole industries have gone broke assuming the Fed was bluffing. It isn't a mood. It's a mandate — by law, this machine chases two numbers: stable prices, and maximum employment. Everything it does is a trade between those two.

[GFX: checklist ×3; dual-gauge "PRICES | JOBS" balancing]

## CLOSE + SEQUEL LOOP (9:55–10:40)

**VO:** Here's the thing about the money machine so far: the bank decides what happens to your deposits. The Fed decides what money costs. But there's a third machine — and this one is pointed directly *at you.* It watches every card swipe and every late payment, compresses your entire financial life into three digits, and sells that number about ten billion times a year — to people deciding whether you get the apartment, the loan, the job. You've spent your whole life being scored by a machine you've never seen. That's the next video.
No hype. Just the gears.
